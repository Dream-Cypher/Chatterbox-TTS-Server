The lighthouse had stood on the point for a hundred and forty years, and in all that time it had gone dark exactly twice. The first was the winter of the great storm, when the supply boat could not land for nine days and the oil ran out on the eighth. The second nobody talked about.

Martha had kept the light for thirty-one of those years, taking over from her father the summer she turned nineteen. She knew the stairs by heart, all one hundred and eighty-two of them, and could climb them in the dark carrying a full can without spilling a drop. Visitors always asked whether she got lonely. She never knew how to explain that the light was company enough, that a thing which needs you every single night without fail is a kind of relationship.

The automation notice came on a Tuesday, in an envelope thin enough that she knew what it said before she opened it. They would fit the new lamp in March. There would be a small ceremony. She was invited to attend as a guest of honour, which struck her as a peculiar way to describe being asked to watch someone take your life's work apart with a screwdriver.

She read it twice, folded it back into thirds, and went up to trim the wick.
